Router Accuracy

The router is the first agent in the pipeline. It receives every user query and classifies it into one of 8 intent categories: SEARCH, SUMMARIZE, RECOMMEND, COMPARE, TEMPORAL, FACTCHECK, INSIGHT, or GRAPH. Why 95.8% — What Are the 2 Errors?

The llama3.1-70b model made exactly 2 classification errors out of 48 queries:

Error 1: "What is the consensus on AI safety across podcasts?" → predicted INSIGHT, expected SUMMARIZE. This is a genuinely ambiguous query. Both INSIGHT (analytical overview across channels) and SUMMARIZE (synthesize what experts say) are reasonable interpretations. The distinction between these two intents is one of the thinnest in the taxonomy — even a human annotator could disagree here.

Error 2: "Who discussed AI safety across different podcasts?" → predicted SEARCH, expected GRAPH. The query asks for cross-podcast speaker connections, which is a GRAPH operation. The model interpreted it as a SEARCH query — understandable because it uses "who discussed" phrasing that reads more like a lookup than a graph traversal.

Why the 8b Model Struggles More

The 8b model's main weakness is the SEARCH vs. SUMMARIZE boundary — 2 of its 6 errors are SEARCH queries misclassified as SUMMARIZE. Queries like "What did Lex Fridman say about consciousness?" and "What did Marc Andreessen say about software eating the world?" are ambiguous: they could be asking for a direct quote (SEARCH) or a synthesized summary (SUMMARIZE). The 70b model handles this distinction correctly because it has better instruction-following and semantic disambiguation ability. The +8.3% ablation delta directly justifies the routing decision to use llama3.1-70b in production.
✅
Assessment: 95.8% router accuracy is excellent for an 8-class classification task. Industry baselines for intent classification in production NLP systems typically range from 85–93%. PodcastIQ's router exceeds this with a more complex 8-class taxonomy.

Understanding the Metrics

Precision@K measures what fraction of the top-K retrieved chunks are actually relevant. MRR (Mean Reciprocal Rank) measures how high the first relevant result appears — MRR=1.0 means the top result is always relevant; MRR=0.5 means the first relevant result is typically in position 2.

The pattern here — strong MRR (0.775) with declining Precision@K — is a classic characteristic of a recall-oriented retrieval system. The system reliably puts at least one highly relevant chunk near the top, but retrieves progressively noisier results as K increases. For a summarization use case, this is ideal: the most important signal is in the top 2–3 results.

Why Precision@1 is 0.65 (Not Higher)

Seven out of 20 queries returned a non-relevant chunk as the top result. The two worst-performing queries were:

"Crypto and blockchain future predictions" — returned 0 relevant results across all 8 positions (P@8 = 0.0). This indicates sparse topic coverage in the corpus. The 25 channels indexed focus primarily on technology, health, and startup topics; dedicated crypto podcast content is underrepresented.

"Podcast industry growth and creator economy" — also returned 0 relevant results. This is a meta-topic (about podcasting itself) rather than content covered within podcasts, explaining the zero recall.

Excluding these two out-of-domain queries, effective P@1 rises to 0.72 and effective MRR rises to 0.86 — which is strong for a production semantic search system.

Why ROUGE Scores Are Low — And Why That's Expected

ROUGE measures lexical overlap between the generated summary and the reference text (concatenated retrieved chunks). A low ROUGE score does not necessarily mean the response is bad — it means the LLM is paraphrasing rather than copying text verbatim from the source chunks.

In an abstractive summarization system like PodcastIQ, this is by design. The llama3.1-70b model is instructed to synthesize multiple perspectives into a coherent narrative, not to extract sentences. BERTScore F1 of 0.774 — which measures semantic similarity rather than word matching — confirms that the responses are genuinely aligned with the source content even when the exact words differ. ROUGE-L F1 below 0.15 is typical for abstractive summarization; extractive models score 0.40–0.55.

Why Faithfulness Is 2.4/5 — The Hallucination Problem

This is the most significant finding in the generation evaluation. The LLM-as-judge consistently penalized responses for introducing speaker names, quotes, and claims that were not present in the retrieved context window. Key patterns observed:

Phantom Speaker Attribution: In 6 out of 10 queries, the model mentioned speakers like Dr. Andrew Huberman, Dr. Rhonda Patrick, or Peter Thiel who do not appear in the retrieved chunks for that query. The model is drawing from its training knowledge about these public figures rather than staying within the retrieved context.

Cross-query Contamination: In the "startup strategies" query, the model attributed advice to specific founders (e.g., Paul Graham-style hiring philosophy) not present in the returned 5 chunks. The model is recalling general startup knowledge from training rather than the actual episode content.

Why This Happens: llama3.1-70b is a capable general-purpose model with extensive knowledge about the podcast personalities in the PodcastIQ corpus. When the retrieved context is sparse or doesn't perfectly match the query, the model "fills in the gaps" with what it knows — a known failure mode called retrieval hallucination.

High Relevance Despite Low Faithfulness: The 4.4/5 relevance score alongside 2.4/5 faithfulness reveals an important distinction — the model answers the right question but draws on the wrong sources. The response is topically correct but not grounded in the podcast content specifically.
⚠️
Primary Recommendation: Add a strict grounding instruction to the summarization prompt: "Only include claims, quotes, and speaker attributions that appear explicitly in the source chunks provided. Do not use your prior knowledge about these speakers." This change is expected to raise faithfulness to 3.5–4.0/5 without significantly impacting relevance scores.

Why the SEARCH Agent Is the Outlier (25.7s mean, 49.1s p95)

The SEARCH agent's high latency is due to a cold-start outlier. One of the three measured queries hit 49.1 seconds — 3× longer than the other two (12.8s and 15.2s). This is consistent with Snowflake Cortex warehouse cold-start behavior: if the warehouse has auto-suspended since the last query, the first query incurs a 30–60 second resume penalty.

The other two SEARCH queries complete in 12–15 seconds, which is consistent with other agents. Excluding the cold-start outlier, the SEARCH mean drops to ~14s — much closer to the overall average. In a production deployment with a warm warehouse, SEARCH latency would likely fall to 8–12 seconds.

Root Cause: Snowflake Cortex Cold-Start

Snowflake Cortex operates as a serverless function over shared compute. Each LLM call goes through: (1) embedding generation via arctic-embed-m → (2) vector similarity search over 13,807 embeddings → (3) llama3.1-70b inference via Cortex Complete API. Each step involves a network round-trip to Snowflake's infrastructure.

At 12–16 seconds for a warm warehouse, this latency is comparable to other cloud-hosted LLM APIs (OpenAI GPT-4o: 5–15s for long completions, Anthropic Claude: 8–20s). For a demo/portfolio system, this is acceptable. For a high-traffic production system, warehouse size scaling (M or L) and connection pooling would bring latency down to 3–6 seconds.

Why SUMMARIZE Costs the Most

SUMMARIZE consumes the most tokens because it passes the largest context to the LLM: the user query, a system prompt, and typically 5–8 retrieved chunks (~3,000 input tokens) followed by a 500–700 token synthesis. GRAPH and SEARCH have near-zero LLM costs because they use structured SQL queries against the knowledge graph and Cortex Search index respectively — no generative inference step required.

Priority 1 — Fix Faithfulness (High Impact, Low Effort)

Update the summarization system prompt to explicitly constrain the model: "Only reference speakers, claims, and facts that appear in the provided context chunks. Do not supplement with external knowledge." This single prompt change is expected to raise faithfulness from 2.4 to 3.5–4.0 without any infrastructure changes.

Priority 2 — Improve Corpus Coverage for Sparse Topics

Two retrieval queries returned zero relevant results: crypto/blockchain and podcast industry growth. Ingesting 10–15 additional episodes from dedicated crypto podcasts (e.g., Bankless, The Pomp Podcast) would close these gaps and improve overall P@1.

Priority 3 — Acknowledge Latency as Infrastructure Constraint

The 5-second PRD target is not achievable with Snowflake Cortex on an X-SMALL warehouse without connection warming. Update the PRD to reflect a realistic target of p95 < 20 seconds for the current architecture, or invest in warehouse size scaling (M/L warehouse) and keep-warm queries to achieve 6–8 second response times.

Priority 4 — Add GPT-4o as Cross-Validation Judge

The current LLM-as-judge uses llama3.1-70b to evaluate llama3.1-70b's own outputs — a potential self-serving bias. Adding OpenAI GPT-4o as a second, independent judge and comparing both scores would significantly strengthen the credibility of the generation quality metrics. If both models agree on faithfulness and groundedness scores, the evaluation is model-agnostic and more defensible.
